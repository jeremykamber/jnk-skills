# The coach interview

Ask these before designing anything, **one at a time**, each with the
recommended answer attached, and stop when the user is done answering.
It is an interview, not an intake form: eleven questions fired at once
get skimmed, and the answers that matter (prior knowledge, the anchor
question) are the ones a person wants to think about.

Order is need-to-know first. Q1–Q3 decide whether a deck is even the
right artefact; Q4–Q7 decide what fits the room; Q8–Q11 write the
brief. Do not skip to Q8 because the user asked for "a slide about X".

**Reasoning vocabulary**: *enduring understanding, evidence of
understanding, prior knowledge, expertise reversal, scalable activity,
anchor question, debrief.*

## Phase 1 — outcome and evidence

**Q1. What should they be able to do afterwards, and what is the one
idea that should survive past the session?**
*Why:* backward design Stage 1 (Wiggins & McTighe 2005). Without it
you cannot tell a slide that earns its place from one that fills a
slot. *Unlocks:* every keep/cut decision, and the mode (teach vs
inform).
*Boundary:* the framework's evidence is practitioner-report, not
controlled — nobody has run the RCT that shows backward-designed
courses beat traditionally-designed ones. It earns its place by
preventing two known errors: coverage-for-its-own-sake, and activities
with no priority.

**Q2. How will you know they got it — what will you see or collect?**
*Why:* Stage 2. Activity without specified evidence is time spent, not
learning produced. *Unlocks:* the activity, its deliverable, and what
the closing prompt asks for.

**Q3. What do they already know, and what do they get wrong about this?**
*Why:* prior knowledge dominates design. The same worked example that
lifts a novice (d ≈ 0.9–1.2) adds nothing and can hurt an expert
(d ≈ −0.33 to −0.75), and across Kalyuga's review the novice/expert
gap in optimal guidance reaches d ≈ 1.0–2.0 (Kalyuga 2007; the 2025
meta-analysis replicates it). *Unlocks:* how much scaffolding, and
whether the deck teaches the thing or corrects the thing.
*Note:* misconceptions do not yield to being told the right answer;
they need to be confronted explicitly, which is what a prompt with a
plausible wrong option does.

## Phase 2 — the room

**Q4. How many people, and do they know each other?**
*Why:* active learning's benefit holds across class size (Freeman et
al. 2014), but the *format* does not scale equally: vote/discuss/revote
works at 300, jigsaw and gallery walks do not. *Unlocks:* peer
instruction, think-pair-share, or table work.

**Q5. How long is the slot, and how much of it is the activity
including its debrief?**
*Why:* an activity's learning is consolidated in the debrief, and a
debrief takes minutes. Mazur's cycle runs 8–15 minutes per question;
think-pair-share needs 5–7 to be worth starting. *Unlocks:* how many
activity cycles fit.

**Q6. Do they have devices and network?**
*Why:* polling can be phones, clickers, or hands — but a QR that
dead-ends on captive-portal wifi is worse than a hand raise. Moving a
task to a device also raises load unless the interface is trivial
(Skulmowski 2024). *Unlocks:* interactive artifact vs on-screen
question vs paper.

**Q7. Anything that would exclude someone — vision, hearing, motor,
processing time?**
*Why:* participation formats each carry an exclusion: rapid polling
excludes some motor profiles, oral-only pair work excludes deaf
students, tight time boxes exclude processing differences.
*Unlocks:* the alternative path, decided now rather than improvised in
the room.

## Phase 3 — the design

*For inform decks, Q8–Q11 are optional. Inform mode generates a clear,
visual briefing by default. Skip to writing the spine unless the user
specifically asks for interactive elements.*

**Q8. Which format: peer instruction, think-pair-share, table task, or
individual practice?**
*Why:* the evidence differs per format. Vote → discuss → revote →
explain is the strongest-supported (Smith et al. 2009). In
think-pair-share the *pair* step is what produces the gain: dropping it
removed the entire participation benefit in a controlled comparison
(Mundelsee & Jurkowski 2021). *Unlocks:* the slide's fields — grouping,
time, deliverable.

**Q9. What is the briefing — purpose, task, time, deliverable, social
configuration?**
*Why:* this is the contract on the slide, and the checker enforces
every field. Stating the *purpose* is widely advised but weakly
evidenced (correlational); stating the *task and format* clearly is
what reduces ambiguity. *Unlocks:* the activity slide itself.

**Q10. What is the anchor question or problem?**
*Why:* the quality of the anchor decides whether discussion is
productive. Design it so the first vote lands between 35% and 70%
correct — disagreement is what makes the discussion happen — with one
plausible distractor per misconception. *Unlocks:* the slide and any
interactive exercise built around the same question.

**Q11. How will you close it — what do you do with their work, and how
does it reconnect to Q1?**
*Why:* debriefing raises performance by roughly 25% relative to no
debrief (Tannenbaum & Cerasoli 2013) and is where your expertise adds
what peer discussion cannot. An activity with no debrief is a break.
*Boundary honesty:* the CMU study found no difference between debrief
types, and Freeman et al. do not separate debriefed from
non-debriefed activities — so treat the debrief as necessary, not as
something whose exact form is settled by evidence.

## What the interview is not

- **Not a survey.** Ask one, propose your answer, let them decide.
- **Not a place to sell a framework.** If the user's slot is ten
  minutes of announcements, the honest answer is "this is an inform
  deck with one prompt", not a redesign.
- **Not a place for the weak claims.** "Active learning always beats
  lecture" is false as stated (badly designed activity can beat
  nothing); "stating the purpose raises engagement" is assumed more
  than shown; "students always learn from peer discussion" ignores the
  23% who stayed wrong after discussing. Use the numbers above, with
  their boundaries.
