# Inform mode

Inform mode is the catch-all: present something clearly so the audience
understands it, remembers it, and knows what it means for them. It is
not optimized for durable skill — that is Teach mode — but it still
uses the multimedia principles, because understanding is what they
protect.

Optimize for clarity, memorability, and engagement. Read
[slide-craft.md](slide-craft.md) first; those rules all apply.

**Reasoning vocabulary**: *spine, headline test, one anchor, the
question, advance organizer, so-what.*

## The spine comes first

Write one sentence: **what should they believe or do after this?**
That sentence is the deck. Everything that does not serve it is cut —
the seductive-details evidence (g = −0.33) is not a warning about
images, it is a warning about material that is interesting and
irrelevant.

Then order it as an argument:

1. **Title** — what this is, for whom.
2. **The question** — the situation or problem, one slide. Open the
   gap before filling it (information gap — Loewenstein 1994). A
   surprising number or a concrete case beats a framing slide.
3. **The point** — the answer, on its own slide, early. Do not save the
   conclusion for the end (pyramid principle — Minto): if the audience
   leaves after five minutes, they should still have the answer.
4. **Support** — two to four sections, each a claim with its evidence.
   One claim per slide, assertion headlines throughout.
5. **So what** — implications, the ask, the next step, who does what.
6. **Close** — the single line they should repeat back.

The **headline test**: read only the slide titles, in order. If that
sequence is the argument, the deck is right. If it reads like a table
of contents, you have labels where claims belong (see
[slide-craft.md](slide-craft.md)).

## Make them guess before you tell them

Before your answer, state the answer the audience would have reached
and why it fails. A refutation — misconception, refutation, correct
explanation — beats the same content presented straightforwardly,
g = 0.41 across 44 comparisons (Schroeder & Kucera 2022), and a
pre-registered meta-analysis of 294 effects confirms it (Danielson
et al. 2025). Three slides carry that structure:

1. the intuitive approach ("everyone patches it"),
2. why it breaks (the failure they will recognize),
3. your answer, which now has somewhere to attach.

This is what an anticipation curve is worth keeping: the payoff is a
*corrected prediction*, and the correction is real content rather than
a tease. A loop you never close is clickbait, and the memory advantage
you were hoping for from it does not exist (slide-craft.md, evidence).

The same move works inside one slide: the two-column comparison is at
its best when one column is the thing they believe and the other is
why it fails.

## Advance organizer, not housekeeping

One slide of "what this covers and why it matters now" earns its place
by giving the audience the structure they will hang the rest on
(Ausubel 1960). A housekeeping slide — who you are, the agenda as a
list of nouns, "please hold questions" — spends attention and returns
nothing. Put logistics in the calendar invite.

## One anchor

The deck should leave one repeatable thing behind: a number, a phrase,
a contrast, a picture of the problem. Choose it deliberately and place
it at the start and at the close; two encounters of the same line
beat one repetition (spacing). The slide that carries its *proof* goes
at the peak, where memory is anchored (slide-craft.md).

Concreteness is what makes an anchor stick:

| Abstract | Concrete |
|---|---|
| "significantly faster" | "40 seconds instead of 4 minutes" |
| "improve retention" | "half as many people cancel in month two" |
| "process inefficiency" | "three people re-typing the same address" |

Stories and single cases carry abstract claims into memory; a claim with
one vivid, *relevant* case outperforms the same claim with an invented
average. Relevant is the operative word — a case that is merely
entertaining is a seductive detail.

## Slides for a room, notes for the reader

A deck is a delivery mechanism, not a document. If someone must be able
to read it alone, that reader gets the notes; the slides stay cues
(slide-craft.md, "the slide is the cue"). If the material genuinely
needs paragraphs, tables and references, it is a document with a deck
summary, not a 60-slide deck.

Keep the room's attention on the point: no animated builds that reveal
nothing, no template ornament, no decorative photography.

## Say what you don't know

State assumptions, limits and uncertainty where they belong — on the
slide that depends on them, in one line. An audience that catches an
unstated assumption stops listening to the argument and starts auditing
it. One line of "what would change this" is cheap and buys credibility.

## Plain delivery, no required interaction

- **Second person, conversational** register outperforms formal
  (personalization principle — Mayer 2021).
- **Relevance**: say why this matters to *this* audience early; if you
  cannot, the deck is aimed at the wrong room.
- **Cut the third-quarter sag**: if a section has nothing the audience
  needs, delete the section rather than compressing it.

The deck is a presentation, not a workshop. Interactive elements —
`prompt` slides, `activity` slides — are available when the user asks
for them, but the default is a clear, visual, self-contained briefing.

## Build checklist

- [ ] One sentence says what they should believe or do
- [ ] The headline test passes: titles in order read as the argument
- [ ] The point appears early, not at the end
- [ ] The answer they would have reached is refuted before your own
- [ ] The strongest slide is at the peak, and the middle has no filler
- [ ] Two to four support sections, one claim each
- [ ] One deliberate anchor, placed at the start and the close
- [ ] Every visual explains a claim; nothing decorative remains
- [ ] Assumptions and limits stated where they matter
- [ ] Ends with the ask / next step / line to repeat
- [ ] `check-deck.mjs` reports no errors
