# Practice design

The rules every exercise obeys, in either mode, and where they come
from. Read this before authoring; the mode reference says *what shape
the practice takes*, this says *what makes it work*.

**Reasoning vocabulary**: *first attempt, elaborated feedback, worked
example, completion problem, fade, interleave, spacing, desirable
difficulty, calibration, second pass.*

## A first attempt is the unit of work

Every step asks for an answer before it offers one. Committing to an
answer you might get wrong is the active ingredient: retrieval practice
beats restudy by about g = 0.50, and the effect *grows* with the
retention interval (Rowland 2014, k = 159) — it pays off in weeks, not
at the end of the session. Practice testing is one of only two
techniques that survive the Dunlosky et al. (2013) review as
high-utility across ages, materials and outcomes; the other is
distributing it over time.

Two consequences for the spec:

- **Ask for production, not recognition.** Free recall and short answer
  beat multiple choice as the *initial* retrieval attempt (Rowland
  2014). Multiple choice earns its place when the distractors are the
  misconceptions you are hunting, not as the default.
- **The first attempt is what the exercise reports.** A second attempt
  still teaches, but it is a different event — count it and guessing
  looks like knowing.

## 2–3 spaced tries per idea, and only the missed ones come back

Optimal spacing is at roughly *equal* intervals, not expanding ones
(Karpicke & Roediger 2006), and 2–3 spaced retrievals per item capture
nearly all the benefit — beyond three the returns shrink sharply
(Karpicke & Roediger 2008). Test-until-correct beats a single attempt,
and the right number depends on how long you want it kept: two attempts
is near-optimal for a week, three for a month (Pyc & Rawson 2010).

So: the second pass re-asks **only the missed steps**, spaced to the end
of the run rather than immediately after the miss. Re-asking everything
is rereading with extra typing.

## Feedback explains, it does not grade

"Correct" and "incorrect" are the weakest useful feedback there is:
right/wrong without elaboration is minimally effective (Van der Kleij et
al. 2015), and in Hattie & Timperley's (2007) synthesis task-level
feedback is the weakest of the four levels while process ("this fails
because the loop runs one time too many") and self-regulation feedback
do the work.

- **Write the distractor's feedback first.** A wrong answer is where the
  misconception lives, so that is where the teaching goes.
- **Immediate**, for procedural steps — a mechanic running tests wants
  the result now (Shute 2008; Bangert-Drowns et al. 1991). Delay is for
  conceptual review, not for a step they just attempted.
- **No praise in the feedback slot.** Praise-level feedback sits around
  d = 0.14 and pulls attention off the task. (It does not *undermine*
  motivation — Deci et al. 1999 found verbal praise harmless — it is
  just weak, and the slot is better spent on the process.)
- **Hints are scaffolds, and they are counted.** Publish the hint count
  at the end, so a student who leaned on them can see the difference
  between following a recipe and knowing it.

## Fade the scaffolding — within the set and across it

Novices learn more from studying a correct worked example than from
solving the same problem cold: g = 0.48 on mathematics outcomes (Chawla
2023, 43 articles), and the classic worked-example effect is larger
still. Guidance should *fade*, not vanish: full example → completion
problem (the last step missing) → the whole thing alone. Fading beats
both full examples and full problem-solving (Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill
2003; Salden et al. 2010).

- Set 1 step that is read-and-explain, one completion problem, then
  problems that are theirs.
- **Do not bolt a self-explanation prompt onto an already-loaded
  example.** Chawla (2023) found examples *with* self-explanation
  prompts did worse than correct examples alone — the prompt is
  extraneous load when the example is not yet understood. Self-
  explanation belongs on the *student's own* attempt (see below), or
  after the example has been studied.
- Stop fading when they are getting similar problems right about 80% of
  the time (practitioner consensus — Rosenshine-style guidance, not a
  measured constant).
- The effect reverses for people who already hold the skill (expertise
  reversal; Kalyuga 2007, replicated meta-analytically in 2025). If the
  audience knows it, skip the example instead of padding the set with a
  walkthrough they will skim.

## Let them struggle first — but only where it pays

Problem-solving *before* instruction beats instruction first: g = 0.43
[0.19, 0.68] across the productive-failure literature (Sinha & Kapur
2021, 53 studies), strongest for complex conceptual tasks and when
scaffolding is available *during* the struggle. Three boundaries:

- **Conceptual, not fluency.** For fact recall and simple procedures,
  direct instruction is more efficient. Do not manufacture a struggle
  over something with one obvious answer.
- **The explanation must follow.** Unsupported failure is wasted time;
  the gain comes from the gap being exposed and then filled.
- **Domain-modulated.** The effect is well established in STEM and has
  failed to replicate in some non-STEM settings.

## Interleave where confusion lives, block where it does not

Interleaving beats blocking by about g = 0.42 (Brunmair & Richter 2019,
59 studies, N = 8,466), and the moderator is *discriminability*: the
gain is largest when the categories are easy to confuse (Taylor &
Rohrer 2010 — 63% vs 20% on a one-week test for interleaved practice of
similar-looking problems). The exception runs the other way: for
vocabulary and definitions, where the shared features *are* the point,
blocking was better (g = −0.39).

So interleave interaction kinds and problem types within a set, and let
the checker's cadence warning tell you when a run of slides or steps is
uniform. Do not interleave a definition drill.

## Make them explain once — on their own attempt

One step per set asks them to produce a short explanation, with the
model answer hidden until they commit. Producing before seeing is the
point; a model answer read first is just more text. Self-explanation
paired with *their own* work is what Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill (2003)
and Renkl's fading studies support; the prompt that hurts is the one
stacked on an unfamiliar example (Chawla 2023).

If you want the metacognitive version, ask **how sure they are before
the reveal** — confidence judgments are what combat the fluency illusion
(Koriat & Bjork 2005; Metcalfe 2009), and the confident error is the one
worth correcting in public. The shipped shell does not ask for
confidence; it reports the first-try count instead. Do not claim a
calibration benefit the shell does not produce.

## Difficulty that is desirable, and difficulty that is just bad

Working at the edge of current ability optimises learning (Metcalfe
2009); ease during practice predicts *worse* retention, because fluency
is mistaken for competence (Koriat & Bjork 2005; Bjork & Bjork). Aim for
a first-try success rate around 60–80% (practitioner consensus): high
enough to stay in it, low enough to be worth doing. The evidence for
self-efficacy has the mirror rule — mastery experiences are the
strongest source of it (Bandura; Täschner et al. 2025), and early
success builds the persistence to survive later difficulty (Multon et
al. 1991).

But *difficulty* is not automatically *desirable*: ambiguous wording,
missing information and unclear instructions are extraneous load that
costs outcomes for nothing (Bjork & Bjork; Skulmowski 2024 for
activities). Slow because the idea is hard: keep. Slow because the
question is muddy: fix.

## Practice is not a test

A test scores you; practice changes you. So:

- **No time pressure on the student's screen, ever.** A time box belongs
  on the activity brief in the deck, not on the exercise.
- **No streaks, points or leaderboards.** Gamification's cognitive
  effect is small (g ≈ 0.49) and its motivational effects are unstable
  (Sailer & Homner 2020); controlling rewards measurably reduce
  intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks (Deci, Koestner & Ryan
  1999, k = 128). Intrinsic motivation predicts the *quality* of
  performance, extrinsic incentives the *quantity* (Cerasoli et al.
  2014) — this is quality work.
- **Support autonomy in the wording**: say why the practice is worth
  doing, offer a choice of route where there is one, avoid controlling
  language. Autonomy support is the strongest single predictor of
  autonomous motivation (Bureau et al. 2021, 144 studies).
- **Show progress, not points.** A position marker and a first-try count
  are honest, cost nothing, and use the goal-gradient effect (effort
  rises as the end comes into view; Kivetz et al. 2006).
- **Say the difficulty out loud on the page**: "this will feel harder
  than rereading — that is what makes it stick". Otherwise the fluency
  illusion wins and they go back to rereading.

## What not to do

- **Do not make it fun instead of hard.** Decorative detail, jokes that
  carry no content, and animation compete with the material for working
  memory.
- **Do not build a mock lattice around internals.** Steps and tests
  assert observable behaviour, so the exercise survives a refactor and
  the student learns the contract rather than your implementation.
- **Do not pad the set.** Four varied steps teach more than ten
  near-identical ones. If the set feels long, cut.
- **Do not assume practice hours predict learning.** Deliberate
  practice explains only ~4% of performance variance in education (and
  <1% in professions) under Macnamara et al.'s (2014) meta-analysis of
  88 studies — structure, feedback and prior knowledge carry the rest.
  The useful part of Ericsson's prescription is the *shape* (a specific
  goal, immediate feedback, edge-of-ability work), not the hours.
- **Do not hide the answers and call it integrity.** A client-side
  exercise is open by construction. Say so, and make the answer panel
  the review sheet.

## Evidence

Read the paper before you bend a rule; effect sizes are quoted as the
source reports them.

- **Rowland 2014** (k = 159) — retrieval practice vs restudy g = 0.50;
  effect grows with retention interval; free recall > recognition.
- **Karpicke & Roediger 2006, 2008; Pyc & Rawson 2010** — equal-interval
  spacing beats expanding; 2–3 spaced retrievals per item capture the
  benefit; test-until-correct with more attempts for longer retention.
- **Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt 2021** (k = 50 classrooms, N = 5,374) —
  retrieval practice works with real course materials; undergrad effects
  run smaller than K-12 or medical.
- **Bangert-Drowns et al. 1991; Shute 2008** — immediate feedback for
  procedural skill, delayed for conceptual; elaborated feedback names
  the gap and the next move.
- **Van der Kleij et al. 2015** — correctness-only feedback is barely
  better than nothing.
- **Hattie & Timperley 2007** — feedback ≈ d = 0.41 overall, but
  task-level is the weakest level, process and self-regulation the
  strongest; praise ≈ d = 0.14.
- **Chawla 2023** (43 articles, 181 effect sizes) — worked examples
  g = 0.48; correct examples beat incorrect ones; self-explanation
  prompts *stacked on* examples hurt.
- **Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill 2003; Salden et al. 2010** — faded worked
  steps plus self-explanation beat full examples and full problems.
- **Kalyuga 2007** — expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices
  (d ≈ 0.9–1.2 for worked examples) becomes inert or harmful for the
  knowledgeable (d ≈ −0.33 to −0.75).
- **Sinha & Kapur 2021** (53 studies) — productive failure g = 0.43
  [0.19, 0.68]; complex conceptual tasks; scaffolding during the
  struggle; explicit instruction must follow.
- **Brunmair & Richter 2019** (N = 8,466) — interleaving g = 0.42
  [0.34, 0.50]; blocking better for vocabulary (g = −0.39);
  discriminability is the moderator. **Taylor & Rohrer 2010** — 63% vs
  20% at one week.
- **Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001; Fazio & Marsh 2009** — high-confidence
  errors are corrected *more* once feedback arrives; expect the
  confident error to be the best teaching moment.
- **Richland & Kornell 2009** — attempting and failing improves later
  learning relative to studying the answer.
- **Koriat & Bjork 2005; Metcalfe 2009** — the fluency illusion, and the
  region of proximal learning: the feeling of difficulty is a better
  guide than the feeling of ease.
- **Bandura 1977; Täschner et al. 2025; Multon et al. 1991** — mastery
  experiences are the strongest source of self-efficacy; self-efficacy
  accounts for ~14% of academic performance variance.
- **Bureau et al. 2021** (144 studies) — autonomy support is the
  strongest predictor of autonomous motivation, competence second.
- **Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999** (k = 128) — tangible controlling
  rewards reduce intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks; verbal
  praise does not.
- **Cerasoli, Nicklin & Ford 2014** (k = 183) — intrinsic motivation
  predicts quality, incentives quantity, and incentives crowd out the
  intrinsic effect when tied to performance.
- **Sailer & Homner 2020** — gamification: cognitive g ≈ 0.49, but
  motivational and behavioural effects unstable.
- **Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald 2014** (88 studies) — deliberate
  practice explains 26% of variance in games, 18% in sports, 4% in
  education, <1% in professions.
- **Freeman et al. 2014** (225 STEM studies) — active learning SMD 0.47;
  failure 33.8% → 21.8%. Publication-bias caveat from the authors;
  the failure-rate finding is the robust part.
